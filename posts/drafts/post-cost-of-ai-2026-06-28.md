Four of last week's biggest tech stories were about the same thing — the cost of AI. From four different angles.

The producer side: OpenAI and Broadcom unveiled Jalapeño on Tuesday — OpenAI's first custom inference chip, taped out in nine months (the fastest ASIC of its class ever), running ~50% cheaper than typical AI GPUs. Gigawatt-scale deployment with Microsoft starts end-2026 and runs through 2029. At the scale Microsoft is committing to, a 50% inference-cost cut is a balance-sheet variable, not a procurement footnote. Vendor pricing built on H100/H200 economics needs re-benchmarking.

The compute-lock side: Qualcomm bought Modular for $3.92 billion in stock — up to 19.2 million Qualcomm shares. Modular's MAX/Mojo stack — built by LLVM founder Chris Lattner — runs AI models across heterogeneous chips. The cleanest CUDA alternative shipped. CUDA isn't just a software moat; it's a pricing moat. A working alternative compresses NVIDIA's pricing power across the industry. Qualcomm is in the AI infrastructure fight now, not just at the edge.

The buyer side: CNBC's Friday read was that enterprise AI buyers are tired of token-volume pitches and rotating to measured efficiency — a direct response to what Palantir's Karp called "tokenmaxxing" two weeks ago. RFP scoring criteria are being rewritten. The procurement question used to be "which model has the bigger context window?" Now it's "which tier of which model on what review tempo gives me the most outcome per dollar?"

The subscription side: Microsoft 365 Copilot Business pricing transitions to permanent SKUs on July 1 — Business Standard with Copilot at $23.50 per user/month, Business Premium at $32. Standalone Copilot Business cut from $30 to $21 list ($18 with annual promo). The promotional-pricing era for enterprise productivity AI is closing. AI SaaS economics are maturing into stable list prices — the seat-economics of the next five years are being set right now.

Four stories. Four layers of the AI cost stack. All four moved in the same five days.

Here's what I'm carrying out of this:

For procurement, the question shifts from "which frontier model" to "which tier at what unit economics." Karp's "tokenmaxxing" was a provocation two weeks ago. This week it's a documented buyer trend with a matching chip (Jalapeño's 50% cut) and a matching compiler ($3.92B CUDA challenger) being built around it.

For build planning, the cost basis under every AI capex thesis just shifted. If Jalapeño-class economics scale, every TCO model from the last 18 months needs a refresh. If Modular delivers cross-silicon parity, NVIDIA-exclusive procurement is no longer the only safe path.

For vendor conversations, the dynamic flips. Pricing assumptions need to defend themselves against a cheaper, more open alternative — not the other way around.

The AI cost conversation just stopped being a story about scale. This week it became a story about discipline.
